Do we still need
differential equations?

Most equations we go looking for will be solved on a machine anyway. So skip the continuum and ask directly for the thing the machine runs — an integrator — then ask what it must satisfy. The constraints turn out to be sharper than expected.

the answer so far

Positivity certifies stability. It does not certify anything else.

A non-negative stencil reaches twelfth order, so stability does not cost accuracy in the diffusive regime. But the certificate is necessary, not sufficient: at its own boundary the only admissible scheme is consistent, positive, stable — and 10¹¹ times less accurate than the same stencil backed off that boundary. A certificate that admits the worst member of its feasible set has to be paired with something else.

The one identity everything hangs on

F3 · F7

A scheme is a pair (geometry, weights). Consistency determines the weights once you fix the geometry — so the whole free content of a numerical scheme is where it looks. And a consistent predictor always has weights summing to one, which makes the following a chain of equivalences rather than a heuristic:

‖w‖₁ = 1⟺ wᵢ ≥ 0⟺ convex combination⟺ discrete maximum principle⟺ errors add

Under recursion each neighbour value carries its own error, so e* = Σwᵢeᵢ + τ. The amplification factor is ‖w‖₁ exactly:

‖w‖₁ = 1   →   |e| ≤ L·τ    linear in the number of levels ‖w‖₁ = 1+δ →   |e| ≤ τ (1+δ)L / δ  geometric

Amended. That bound is a worst case over adversarial error patterns, and it is generically nowhere near attained. Lax–Wendroff at ν = 0.4 has ‖w‖₁ = 1.24 — my bound predicts blow-up — yet its symbol has modulus exactly 1, and 512-fold composition reaches ‖w‖₁ = 1.59 against a bound of 6.8 × 10⁴⁷. Composition expels the negative lobes. So:

Positivity is the certificate you can always compute; the symbol is the sharp criterion you can only sometimes compute. Scattered meshfree geometry has no symbol, so positivity is the only certificate available there — at the real cost of rejecting perfectly stable schemes like Lax–Wendroff. That cost belongs in the open, not hidden.

What does survive intact: the CFL condition was never really a constraint on the time step. Classical FTCS at the benchmark gives w = [0.4727, 0.1000, 0.4273] — all non-negative, ‖w‖₁ = 1.000000 — and the textbook condition r ≤ ½ is precisely the condition that the middle weight stays non-negative. Positivity, upwind Courant ≤ 1 and cell Péclet ≤ 2 all come back as positivity boundaries to six digits.

This explains, in retrospect, nearly everything measured here. At the frontier s = mΔx/√(2ατ) = 1 by construction, and s = 1 is exactly where every accuracy sweep found its worst results. It is the same pathology found independently from the other side: at the frontier the m residue classes never exchange information, so the scheme is m decoupled coarse solves — which is why every Pareto-optimal configuration sits at s = 3–6.

The rule that follows. Do not take the largest positivity-feasible k. Take the largest k with s ≥ s*, where the measured interior optimum s* ≈ √(2 ln 1/ε) supplies exactly the accuracy criterion the certificate lacks. Two results from different threads compose into one usable design rule — and neither is sufficient alone.

A claim I retracted

F3 · F7 · superseded

This page first argued that positivity caps a scheme at second order, via a Jensen argument: non-negative weights summing to one are a probability distribution, so Σwᵢ Δtᵢ² ≥ (Σwᵢ Δtᵢ)² > 0, and the utt error term can never be cancelled. The inequality is true. The consequence does not follow, and the conclusion is false.

Under a PDE constraint utt is not an independent error term. Because ∂ₓ commutes with the generator, every time derivative collapses into two numbers — q = Δx − cΔt and a = αΔt — and the ½Δt²utt contribution is redistributed across uxx, uxxx and uxxxx, where the remaining moments cancel it routinely. The Jensen constraint binds for the generic hierarchy — exactness for arbitrary smooth functions of x and t — and is vacuous for the PDE-constrained one.

The counterexample is a scheme everyone already knows. Plain FTCS at r = 1/6:

Worse for the original claim: compared at matched moment conditions positivity costs nothing at all — identical convergence rates at order 3 and order 4, and at order 3 the positive stencil is 2.6× more accurate. The 20–50× penalty reported earlier was an artefact of comparing at mismatched conditions, where a symmetric min-norm solution collects a free extra order.

The thing an agent chooses is exactly the thing positivity constrains.

Why learning is forced, not fashionable

F4

For hyperbolic problems no linear scheme is both monotone and high-order — Godunov's theorem, confirmed here at 3, 5, 7 and 11 points for every Courant number. The classical escape has always been to give up linearity: flux limiters, ENO, WENO — schemes that choose their stencil from the local solution. An agent selecting geometry from local state is exactly such a nonlinear scheme, learned instead of hand-designed.

The parabolic case needs a different argument, since positivity costs no order there. What it costs is feasibility, and the feasible set is shaped: one-sided geometries are excluded outright, admissible depth saturates, and where the boundary sits depends on the local cell Péclet number. A fixed rule cannot track a boundary that moves with the solution. That, rather than an order barrier, is the honest case for learning.

Positivity also draws the line between when you can plan and when you must search. With ‖w‖₁ = 1 errors are additive, so the optimal schedule is a shortest-path problem and dynamic programming solves it exactly. With ‖w‖₁ > 1 accumulation is multiplicative and history-dependent, the objective stops factorising, and search becomes necessary rather than merely convenient.

Moments replace Taylor rows

F6

Three Taylor rows are only consistent for a small step; widening the stencil adds freedom but no constraints, so accuracy plateaus. The fix is to match more moments of the propagator — and those come from the PDE coefficients alone, by a closed triangular hierarchy obtained by integrating by parts. No fundamental solution is used anywhere:

dMq/dτ = α·q(q−1)·Mq−2 + c·q·Mq−1,    Mq(0) = δq0

Verified against the closed form to 1.6 × 10⁻⁹ through q = 8. It extends to any linear PDE and, locally, to variable and state-dependent coefficients. The Taylor rows are the q ≤ 2 case — so the moment formulation subsumes and corrects them.

What positivity actually costs

F23 · corrected by F33

Coarse-graining a scheme in space by a factor M admits an exact, finite memory of M − 1 lags. An earlier version of this page reported that memory then buys back positivity geometrically, and that at small M the price is exactly zero. Two of those three claims needed correcting, and the correction is sharper than what it replaces.

The trade curve terminates at M = 3. A non-negative coarse law of any depth or width requires every unresolved alias at the constant coarse mode to satisfy g ≤ 0 — a sign condition. For this scheme that reads r ≥ 1/(4 sin²(π/M)): 0.25 at M = 2, 0.333 at M = 3, and 0.50 at M = 4 — which the scheme's own stability limit forbids. So past M = 3 the positivity defect decays toward an infimum it never attains. There is no trade; there is an obstruction.

Seven of seven against a threshold table measured before the obstruction was derived. And it inverts the reading this page previously carried. At r = 0.10 the discarded mode has g = +0.6 — decaying cleanly, perfectly well-behaved — and it is fatal at every memory depth. At r = 0.45 it has g = −0.8 — barely decaying, but oscillating — and it is free.

Coarse-graining preserves a maximum principle· when the modes you discard oscillate,· and destroys it when they merely decay.

So scale separation is not what makes hierarchies cheap — which was the hypothesis this whole phase was built on. Nor can you buy cheap hierarchies in small steps: decimating by 2 twice retains the same index set as decimating by 4, so memory tracks the total ratio, path-independently. That was to have been the phase's central experiment; it needed no experiment.

Also retracted from this section: the "pure-delay" structure (B₁ = B₂ = 0) reported here as a striking positivity phenomenon is a reach artefact — the coarse map is exactly diagonal for j < M because the fine stencil reaches one cell while coarse cells sit M apart, so the law cannot depend on neighbours before lag M for purely geometric reasons. And the exact M − 1 lags are not special structure but the trivial worst case, attained by any decimation.

Ruled out

kept deliberately

Four claims died this session, three of them mine. They are here because a programme that only records what survived cannot be trusted about what did.

under-determined

"Attend to whatever lets you forget" does not pick out anything

The sentence this programme is built on, measured and found under-determined. Every 20-dimensional invariant subspace of the test system reaches zero memory exactly — including the fastest one. Memory depth is a rank condition, blind to eigenvalue magnitude, so minimising memory is strictly weaker than maximising predictive content and its optimal set is the whole Grassmannian of invariant subspaces. Forgetting is cheap in very many directions; what selects the useful ones is what you retain, not how Markovian you become.

retracted

Positivity does not cap a scheme at second order

Mine, and it was this page's headline. Two independent kills. FTCS at r = 1/6 is non-negative and fourth-order. And the Pawula argument I built on conflates two different conditions: Pawula forbids the stencil's own cumulants from terminating, while accuracy only requires finitely many moments to be matched. Positive stencils matching moments to p = 12 are non-negative throughout (min weight 2.2 × 10⁻⁴) and reach 6.9 × 10⁻¹⁵. Positivity is close to free in the parabolic regime; the classical result that genuinely caps order is Godunov's, and it is hyperbolic.

ruled out

A wide stencil is not the composition it "compresses"

Mine. I told the composition thread these were two representations of one operator. On an identical 81-point footprint at L = 40: composite 3.7 × 10⁻⁶, single wide stencil at p = 8 2.3 × 10⁻¹¹ — better by 1.6 × 10⁵. At p = 2 the wide stencil is instead worse than the composite. Same footprint, different operators; which wins depends entirely on how many moments are matched.

ruled out

The advective accuracy floor was a bug in my own reference

A 3.8 × 10⁻⁷ floor that survived every stencil width, moment order and solver tolerance — and afflicted the exact heat kernel identically, which was the clue. Cause: u_true(x, 0) reconstructed the initial condition through an undamped transformed sine series, so every stencil was fed a bad input while being scored against an exact reference. Six wrong diagnoses first. Fixed; the headline numbers below improved by orders of magnitude as a result.

amended

‖w‖₁ = 1 is sufficient for stability, not necessary

Also mine. The ℓ¹ bound is a worst case that composition does not realise — Lax–Wendroff is the counterexample. Positivity keeps its real advantages (exact at every depth, no translation invariance needed, computable on scattered points) but it is a conservative certificate, and it rejects good schemes.

ruled out

The wide positive stencil is Runge–Kutta–Chebyshev

Not similar to — identical. Running an actual RKC1 recurrence with s = m stages against the frontier stencil agrees to 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁶. Same Δt, same footprint, same cost. There is no new method on that axis.

ruled out

Jet recovery by moment regression is moving least squares

An exact algebraic identity, not a resemblance: ‖dmoment − dWLS‖∞ ≈ 5 × 10⁻¹⁶ for every sample count from 10 to 20 000. The randomisation is strictly a cost. And the premise that averaging over stencils beats finite differences under noise is false as stated — tuning the FD bandwidth erases the gap entirely.

ruled out

The frontier scheme's apparent speed-up was an artefact

At the positivity frontier the m sub-lattices never exchange information. The scheme is m independent coarse finite-difference solves run redundantly, at 7–15× the cost of just coarsening the grid.

corrected

Conditioning is not uninformative about ‖w‖₁

My claim; wrong. The correlation is 0.822. The accurate statement is narrower: the cond < 1e4 threshold is a rank guard, not an amplification guard — it rejects 2 stencils in 15 948, both rank-deficient, while stencils with ‖w‖₁ up to 37 pass straight through.

Fixed in the codebase

15 files

The third consistency row was wrong. Substituting the PDE recursively also reduces uxt and utt to x-derivatives, which contributes two terms the original dropped:

was    ½Δx² + αΔt exact  ½(Δx − cΔt)² + αΔt  =  ½ξ² + αΔt,  ξ = Δx − cΔt

Row 2 is already the characteristic offset ξ, so the corrected row 3 is just the diffusive moment condition in ξ — in the frame moving at speed c the equation is pure diffusion. At the benchmark it moves results by under 0.2%, but with the correction the three-point centred stencil is exactly Lax–Wendroff, where the old row gave FTCS-central, which is unconditionally unstable. The old row could not express a second-order advection scheme at all.

Found by the positivity thread. It also removes a silent failure: the old row was affine in Δt, so a stencil with every neighbour at one x collapsed to rank 2 and lstsq quietly returned Σw = 0.478. ξ² is quadratic in Δt, restoring full rank.
